If you are thinking about suicide

  • You are not alone. Millions of people have felt this way and gone on to heal.
  • Talking to someone — a friend, a doctor, or a crisis counselor — is a sign of strength, not weakness.
  • There is help. Please reach out today.

Warning signs to notice

Suicide is rarely a sudden decision. It usually builds slowly, and the signs are often visible to the people who care. Knowing them can help you start the conversation.

What people may say

  • “I don’t want to be here anymore.”
  • “I’m a burden to everyone.”
  • “It wouldn’t matter if I disappeared.”
  • Giving away valued possessions or saying goodbye.

What people may feel

  • Hopelessness, emptiness, or being trapped.
  • Anxiety, agitation, or rage.
  • Withdrawing from people they care about.
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy.

What people may do

  • Pulling back from friends, family, or work.
  • Talking about death, dying, or being a burden.
  • Risky or impulsive behaviour.
  • Researching methods or saying goodbye.

One sign is enough reason to ask. Asking “Are you thinking about suicide?” does not put the idea in someone’s head — it opens the door to honest conversation.

How to help someone you care about

You do not need to be a therapist. You need to be present, direct, and willing to stay.

1

Ask directly

“Are you thinking about suicide?” Asking clearly and calmly gives them permission to tell you the truth. It does not plant the idea.

2

Listen without fixing

Let them talk. Don’t rush to give advice, correct feelings, or share your own story. Reflect what you hear: “Thank you for trusting me.”

3

Take it seriously

Never keep a secret about suicide, and don’t promise to keep it. Promise instead that you’ll help them get through it.

4

Help them get help

Offer to make the call or sit with them. Call 988 with them. If they are in immediate danger, call 911 and stay until help arrives.

5

Stay connected

Recovery is not linear. Checking in regularly — even a short text — makes the difference after a crisis.

6

Look after yourself

Supporting someone in crisis is hard. Ask for support, use the resources below for yourself, and take breaks. You matter too.

About Hopeline

Hopeline is a community suicide prevention organization. We train volunteers and community leaders to notice the signs, speak up, and stay connected — and we run a free crisis support line for anyone who needs it.

We believe no one should have to navigate a mental health crisis alone, and that the people closest to a person are often the first to notice a crisis is beginning. Our work is about being there early, and being there again after.

Our commitments

  • Confidential — what you share stays with us.
  • Free — no insurance, no cost, no catch.
  • Non-judgmental — no lectures, no blame.
  • Trained — every counsellor completes accredited training.
  • Evidence-informed — our programs follow proven prevention practices.
